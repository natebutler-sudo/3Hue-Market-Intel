01

What ships first

A 90-day sequence, ordered by what unblocks what. The campaign is built around one trigger — a deal frozen behind a security review — because it is the most common, the most urgent, and the one where 3HUE's proof is strongest.

Days 1–14

The trust page and the questionnaire teardown

A firm that sells provability has no public security page. Fix that first — it is simultaneously a credibility repair, an SEO asset and the exact artifact the Hue 01 buyer asks for. Build the lead magnet alongside it.

Owner: marketing · Blocks: the landing page, the outreach sequence
Days 7–21

Landing page and website framework fix

Publish the campaign page, and in the same pass add the framework names to the services pages. NIST, ISO 27001, CIS, SOC 2 and FFIEC currently appear nowhere public, which makes a whole category of intent traffic unable to find the firm.

Owner: marketing · Depends on: Message Stack
Days 14–90

Founder posting cadence

Weekly, from Andrew's profile, not the company page. His profile has roughly three and a half times the following and all of the reach; the company page has no visible posts. Distribution is the constraint, not content.

Owner: Andrew, with drafting support · Cadence: one post per week, minimum
Days 21–45

Discovery script and one-pagers

Nothing converts if the call after the click is improvised. Script first, then the four offer one-pagers, so the same argument survives from ad to close.

Owner: sales, with Andrew · Depends on: Message Stack
Days 30–60

Case study rebuild

Three published stories with strong outcomes, no quotes, no timeframes, no named references. Rebuilt properly they are the strongest proof the firm owns.

Owner: marketing · Requires: client consent, which is the long pole
Ongoing

Outreach to the target list

Small, precise, trigger-aware. Volume is not the play here — a hundred well-chosen companies where a trigger is visible beats two thousand cold.

Owner: sales · Depends on: lead magnet, discovery script

Two things not on this list, because they are decisions rather than tasks: whether to publish pricing, and who owns distribution long-term. Both belong to Andrew, and both change what this campaign can promise.

02

The lead magnet

Not a whitepaper. The most quantified pain in the entire research corpus is the security questionnaire — buyers report 200+ questions, five to ten arriving per week at scale, and roughly $2 of internal cost per question answered. That is a problem with a number attached, which makes it the right thing to lead with.

Security Questionnaire Teardown

Send us the questionnaire that is currently sitting on someone's desk. Within three business days you get back: which questions you can answer today, which ones you cannot and why, which are asking the same thing three different ways, and which two or three genuinely require work before you can answer honestly.

Why it works: it is free, it takes the prospect ten minutes, it removes an actual burden the same week, and it hands 3HUE a complete picture of their control gaps — which is a scoped Snapshot proposal in everything but name.

Why not a whitepaper

  • The market is saturated with compliance content and the buyer is not reading it.
  • A teardown produces a conversation with an artifact in it; a download produces an email address.
  • It demonstrates the core differentiator — scope negotiation, telling them what they don't have to do — instead of describing it.
  • It qualifies hard: a company with a live questionnaire has a live trigger by definition, which is 25 of the 100 points on the fit model.

Guardrail: cap it. Three per week, first come. Free work that is unlimited stops reading as generous and starts reading as idle.

03

Landing page copy

Complete and ready to publish. Written for the Hue 01 buyer at the moment a deal freezes. Note what it does not do: it does not open with a breach statistic, it does not say "compliance is not security," and it names no number that is not in the claims library.

Hero
Your buyer isn't asking whether you're secure. They're asking whether you can prove it.
We build the security program your enterprise deal is waiting on — and then we run it, so the evidence is still there next quarter.
Book the Snapshot — 10 business days
The problem — three short blocks

The contract is sitting in someone's security review. They have asked for a SOC 2 Type II, or an ISO 27001 certificate, or sent a 200-question review with a due date. You are not going to talk your way past it. Sixty-one percent of companies were required to hold a certification to win or renew a contract, and thirty-eight percent had already lost revenue over it before they acted.

You know what needs doing. You don't have anyone to do it. That is not a failure of seriousness — sixty-eight percent of organizations have one or fewer full-time security people while ninety-three percent call security a top priority. The framework is public and free. The person to run it is neither.

And the tool you already bought hasn't finished the job. A compliance platform tells you what is wrong. It does not close the gap, answer the questionnaire, or negotiate scope with your auditor. Keep it — we work on top of it.

What you get

Ten business days to a decision you can act on

The Snapshot is a fixed-scope assessment against the standard your buyer is actually asking for — not a maturity score. You finish it with three things:

  • A straight answer on whether you'll pass, and what has to change first if you won't.
  • A scoped plan with a date — how long, what it costs, who does the work, and what you can tell procurement this week.
  • The list of things you don't have to do. Most of the cost in this work comes from scope nobody questioned.

If you want us to run the program after that, we do. If you'd rather take the plan and run it yourself, that also works — you'll have something an internal hire can inherit rather than start over.

Proof

Transit Technologies came to us with no formal security program and enterprise customers asking questions. They finished with a SOC 2 Type I with zero findings, then a multi-entity Type II across their software portfolio.

A large North American bank needed cloud governance that would hold up under examination. We certified more than a thousand systems under a single scalable assessment framework.

Our founder spent his career closing FDA 483 observations and remediating bank Matters Requiring Attention under sustained audit scrutiny. The person who scopes your engagement is the person who delivers it.

Objections, answered on the page

"We already use Vanta." Good. Keep it. We're the layer that operates it, and you'll get more out of the licence you're already paying for.

"Isn't this just compliance theater?" Often, yes. A certificate says controls existed during a window. That's why we sell the program and not the certificate — you get the report because your buyer needs it, and a program because you do.

"Can you guarantee we'll pass?" No, and be careful with anyone who says otherwise. What we can do is tell you in week two whether you'll pass, instead of letting the auditor tell you in month six.

"Should we just hire someone?" Eventually, yes. A full-time security leader runs $250,000 to $400,000 and takes three to six months to find. We run the program now and build it so that hire inherits something.

Closing CTA
Send us the questionnaire.

If a security review is what's holding up your deal, send it over. Within three business days you'll get back what you can answer today, what you can't, and what actually needs to change — at no cost, and with no obligation to work with us.

Send the questionnaire

Page notes. One CTA, repeated — the Snapshot at the top for prospects who already know they need help, the teardown at the bottom for those who don't yet. No form fields beyond name, work email and the file. Every statistic on the page needs its source visible in small print; an uncited number in front of this buyer does more harm than no number.

04

Founder post series

Six posts, one a week, from Andrew's profile. The format is drawn from what actually performs in this market: a heretical or specific opening line as the first sentence — LinkedIn shows that line as the preview, so it is the headline — then the reasoning, then a question. No hashtag walls, no company-page cross-posting until the profile has momentum.

Post 1 · The thesis

Most security programs don't fail because nobody cared. They fail because nobody designed them to run.

I've spent a career inside real failure conditions — FDA 483 observations, bank MRAs, audits where the margin for error was zero. The pattern never changed. The controls existed. The policies existed. Nobody was operating them between the audits.

A framework tells you what should exist in a mature program. It does not tell you how to engineer or run one. That responsibility belongs somewhere else, and in most organizations it belongs to nobody.

Sixty-eight percent of organizations have one or fewer full-time security people. Ninety-three percent say security is a top priority. Both of those are true at the same time, in the same companies, and that gap is the whole problem.

If you run security somewhere: is your program designed to operate, or designed to pass?

Post 2 · Written in the buyer's objection voice

"We already pay for a compliance platform. Why are we still not done?"

I hear this monthly, and the answer isn't that the tool is bad. The tool is doing exactly what it was built to do.

It finds gaps. It does not close them. It generates a task list. It does not negotiate with your auditor about whether that task is actually in scope. It collects evidence. It does not answer the 200-question security review that just landed in your sales team's inbox.

Those are people jobs. The software was never going to do them, and the disappointment isn't a product failure — it's a category confusion.

Keep the platform. The question worth asking is who operates it.

Post 3 · The anonymized failure narrative

A company files a cyber insurance claim after a breach.

The insurer sends someone to verify the controls that were attested to on the application. What they find does not match what was written down.

Not because anyone lied. Because the person who filled out the form had a general sense of the security posture rather than a verified picture of it — and a general sense is not what the application was asking for.

This is the part people miss about continuous monitoring. It isn't about dashboards. It's about being able to demonstrate, on the worst day, that what you said was true was actually true.

Does what your policy says match what you could demonstrate today?

Post 4 · The number in the first line

86.8% of small businesses have deployed multi-factor authentication. 51.1% require it across all their key accounts.

That gap is the entire story of why compliant organizations still get breached.

Same pattern everywhere you look. 88.4% have backups. 61.4% have tested them. The control is bought, installed, and checked off. Whether it is actually operating is a different question, and it is almost never the question being asked.

Deployed is not operating. An auditor checking a control exists will pass you. An attacker checking whether it is enforced will not.

Source: National Cybersecurity Alliance with CISA, 2026 Small Business Survey, n=1,000.

Post 5 · The contrarian rebuttal

"Frameworks are static, compliance is broken, throw it all out."

I keep seeing this take and it sounds forward-thinking. It's also wrong, and it's wrong in a way that costs organizations real money.

Frameworks were never meant to run a security program. They provide structure — control objectives, shared language, governance expectations. What they don't provide is architecture, risk prioritization, or operational discipline. Those are separate layers, and when they're missing the framework gets blamed for a program design failure.

Criticism is easy. Building the layers underneath is the actual work: architecture that enforces the control, risk management that decides which ones matter, operations that run them daily.

The framework isn't the enemy. It was never the whole program.

Post 6 · The offer, earned by five posts of substance

If a security questionnaire is sitting on someone's desk at your company right now, send it to me.

I'll tell you which questions you can answer today, which you can't and why, which ones are asking the same thing three different ways, and the two or three that genuinely need work before you can answer them honestly.

Three business days. No cost. No obligation to work with us afterward.

I'm doing this because these reviews cost teams roughly two dollars a question in internal time, some companies are answering five to ten of them a week, and most of that effort is spent on questions that were never material in the first place.

Three per week, first come. My DMs are open.

Cadence discipline. The value here is the repetition, not any single post — the practitioners who own this space on LinkedIn did it with years of consistent volume on one point of view, not one viral hit. Six posts is the pilot. If the first three land, the answer is to keep going, not to change format. And react to news within 48 hours when something relevant breaks; those posts consistently outperform planned content.

Outreach sequence

Four touches over three weeks, to a small list where a trigger is visible. Each message is short enough to read on a phone and leads with the prospect's situation rather than 3HUE's credentials.

Touch 1 · Day 1 · Email

Subject: the security review on the [Company] deal

[First name] —

You're selling into enterprise accounts, and I'd guess at least one of them has asked for a SOC 2 report or sent a long security questionnaire in the last quarter.

If that's slowing a deal down, send me the questionnaire. I'll come back within three days with what you can answer today, what you can't, and the two or three things that actually need to change. No cost, and no obligation.

If nothing's blocked right now, say so and I'll leave you alone — I'd rather be useful when it matters.

Andrew

Touch 2 · Day 5 · LinkedIn connection note

[First name] — I write about why security programs pass audits and still fail. Given what [Company] sells and who you sell it to, you probably live this. Happy to be a useful contact whether or not we ever work together.

Touch 3 · Day 10 · Email, value-only

Subject: the scope question nobody asks

[First name] —

One thing worth knowing before you start a SOC 2, whether or not you ever talk to us.

Most of the cost in these engagements comes from scope nobody questioned. Your auditor's requirements are more negotiable than they appear, and compliance platforms tend to recommend engineering work that the standard does not actually require. Teams routinely spend months on controls that were never in scope for the report their customer asked for.

So before you commit: get someone to tell you what you don't have to do. That conversation is usually worth more than the tool.

Andrew

Touch 4 · Day 21 · Email, close the loop

Subject: closing the loop

[First name] — I'll stop here so I'm not cluttering your inbox.

If a customer, an insurer or an investor puts you on a clock, the questionnaire teardown offer stands and my inbox is open. Otherwise, good luck with [specific thing about their business].

Andrew

Rules for this sequence. Never more than four touches. Always give the prospect an easy way to say "nothing is blocked" — the fit model treats no trigger as a disqualifier, so a fast no is a win, not a loss. And send from Andrew, not a shared marketing address: the entire differentiator is that a practitioner is on the other end.

06

Discovery script

Eleven questions, in this order, mapped to the fit model so the call produces a score rather than an impression. The first four decide whether the rest of the call is worth having. Ask them before pitching anything.

"What made you take this call now?"
Criterion 1 · Forcing function · 25 pts

Listening for: a named customer, a renewal date, an exam letter, a diligence process. Follow with "who's asking, and by when?" until you have a name and a date.

Red flag: "we've been meaning to get to this." That is a Tier B at best. Do not spend the hour.

"Who owns security today, and what else do they own?"
Criterion 2 · Ownership gap · 15 pts

Listening for: "our IT director handles it," "the CTO, when he has time," or a pause. The second half of the question does the work — it surfaces that security is one of twenty jobs.

Red flag: a CISO with a team. Different engagement, probably not this one.

"Besides you, who has to be comfortable with this before it happens?"
Criterion 3 · Buyer access · 12 pts

Listening for: whether the economic buyer is reachable. If the answer is the CEO or an operating partner, ask to include them in the next call as a condition of proceeding.

Red flag: "I'll take it to them." Scope negotiation cannot happen through a proxy.

"What happens to the business if this doesn't get resolved?"
Trigger severity · qualifies the budget

Listening for: a number. A stalled contract value, a renewal at risk, a deal already lost. This is the figure the proposal gets priced against — not a tool subscription.

"How often do security questionnaires come in, and who fills them out?"
Criterion 5 · Provability demand · 10 pts

Listening for: frequency and, more importantly, irritation. This is the highest-emotion pain in the market and the natural opening for the teardown offer.

"What have you already bought to solve this, and who runs it?"
Criterion 7 · Tools without an operator · 8 pts

Listening for: a platform they pay for and nobody operates. That is the strongest possible qualifier. Respond with "keep it" — never with a replacement pitch.

"Walk me through where your data actually lives."
Criterion 6 · Environment · 8 pts

Listening for: multi-cloud, OT, multiple entities, AI in production. Complexity is what makes this worth paying a firm for rather than buying software.

"Is this one audit, or is it now an annual cycle? How many legal entities?"
Criterion 8 · Recurring surface · 10 pts

Listening for: whether this becomes an operated program or ends as a project. Multi-entity structures are the difference between an engagement and an annuity.

"Are you using AI anywhere a customer would care about? Has anyone asked yet?"
Cross-sell · the fastest-opening lane

Listening for: AI in the product or in customer-facing decisions with no governance behind it. Eighty percent of companies using AI already field customer questions about AI risk. If they haven't yet, they will.

"When does your cyber insurance renew, and who fills in the application?"
Secondary trigger · often unprompted

Listening for: a date and an uncertain answer to the second half. That combination is the attestation-versus-reality conversation, and it converts even when the compliance conversation stalls.

"If we got this right, what would be true in ninety days that isn't true today?"
The close · sets the success criteria in their words

Listening for: the outcome to write into the proposal verbatim. Whatever they say here is what the engagement is actually being judged on.

Qualify out fast, and say so

If there is no trigger, no path to the economic buyer, or the prospect is clearly shopping for the cheapest certificate, end the call early and honestly: "I don't think you need us yet, and I'd rather tell you that than sell you something."

This wins more business over twelve months than working the deal would. The market is small, practitioners talk, and being the firm that turned work down is worth more than the engagement.

Never say on a first call

  • Any promise about passing an audit.
  • A price before scope. Give a band and the variables that move it.
  • A client name that is not already public.
  • Any figure not in the claims library.
  • Criticism of the platform they already bought.
  • A maturity level. They do not know what it means and it makes the conversation abstract.

08

Case study rebuild

Three stories are published today with genuinely strong outcomes and almost none of the elements that make a case study persuade. Rebuilt to the structure below they become the firm's strongest asset; left as they are they read as claims.

What's missing today

  • No client quotes. The most persuasive sentence in any case study is the client's, not the firm's.
  • No timeframes. "SOC 2 Type I with zero findings" is strong. "In fourteen weeks, from no formal program" is far stronger.
  • No before state with numbers. What was failing, how many controls were open, how long questionnaires were taking.
  • No named reference. Even one buyer willing to take a call is worth more than three anonymous stories.
  • No stated trigger. Every one of these started because someone forced it. That is the part the next buyer recognizes themselves in.

The structure to rebuild to

  1. The trigger. Who asked, for what, by when.
  2. The constraint. Team size, security headcount, what else was competing for attention.
  3. What we scoped out. The differentiator, made concrete — what they didn't have to do.
  4. What we did. Three or four sentences, plainly.
  5. The result, with a date attached. Outcome, elapsed time, findings count.
  6. What it unblocked. The contract, the renewal, the exam, the exit.
  7. One client sentence. Ideally about the founder being on the engagement.

Practical note: consent is the long pole, not writing. Ask for the quote and the reference at the moment the engagement succeeds, not six months later — and ask for permission to name the client in the same conversation. Until consent exists, "a large North American bank" is the correct phrasing and nothing should imply more.

What to measure

Six numbers. Deliberately few, because a small team measuring twenty things measures none of them. Three are leading indicators you can move next week; three tell you whether the motion is actually working.

Founder post cadenceDistribution is the binding constraint. Everything else is downstream of whether posts actually ship.One per week, sustained for a quarter. Miss two in a row and the experiment is over.
Comment-to-reaction ratio on trigger postsReactions measure reach; comments measure whether the argument landed with people who have the problem.Rising ratio over time, and comments from titles that match the ICP rather than from peers in the industry.

The measurement blocker: none of the website-side numbers can be tracked until someone connects Search Console and Analytics and shares access. That is a fifteen-minute task and it is currently blocking the ability to say anything factual about which pages, campaigns or keywords work. Do it before the landing page ships, not after.
